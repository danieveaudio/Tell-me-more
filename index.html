Before We Meet

Hi. I'm Dani. This is not a test, and there are no wrong answers. Truly. You can be messy, half-formed, contradictory, and uncertain in here. That's the good stuff. That's what I work with.

I built this form because I want our first conversation to be useful from the first minute (not spent on logistics or backstory you could have told me in advance). Your answers help me see you more clearly before we sit down together.

How long will this take? Somewhere between 15 and 45 minutes, depending on how deep you want to go. Some people breeze through. Some people have revelations. Both are fine.

What happens with your answers? They come to me (Dani) by email once you press send. Nobody else sees them. I read every word, and I come to our session with a preliminary read already forming. Please send them back by the day before our session.

Can I come back to it? Yes. Your answers save automatically in your browser. Close the tab, come back later, everything will still be here. On a shared or work computer, there's a button on the last screen to clear them.

Do I have to answer everything? The only required field is your email (so I can reach you). Everything else is optional. But the more honest material you give me, the more useful our conversation will be.

There are some breathing tools built into this form. They are not decorative. They are here because your nervous system affects the quality of your thinking, and I want you thinking clearly. Use them or skip them, your call.

Question 1 of 12

It's a Tuesday, two years from now. Your life is working. Describe the day.

Not the highlight reel. A regular day. Where are you? What does the morning feel like? Who's around? What kind of work are you doing (and not doing)? Be specific. Be honest about what you actually want, not what sounds impressive.

It is. Most people have never been asked to describe what they actually want without dressing it up for someone else.

Try this: Close your eyes for ten seconds. Picture yourself waking up on that Tuesday. Don't edit. What's the first thing you see? What do you hear? Now open your eyes and write whatever came up, even if it surprised you. If you don't see pictures when you close your eyes, that's fine, just describe the day in whatever way it comes, words, sounds, how your body feels, a list.

Question 2 of 12

What are you building (or trying to build)?

A business, a practice, a creative project, a career pivot, a side thing that might become the main thing. Whatever it is, tell me about it in your own words. Messy is fine. "I don't know yet" is a real answer.

If you picked "not sure what yet" or "exhausted," this might feel like the hardest question on here. That's okay. Tell me what you're drawn to, what you keep thinking about, or what you'd try if failure wasn't a factor.

That's actually a useful starting point. "I don't know" means you haven't committed to the wrong thing yet.

Try this instead: What do people keep asking you for help with, even when you haven't offered? What topic can you talk about for an hour without getting bored? What would you build if you knew it would work? Write any of those and we'll find the thread together.

What's Real
Question 3 of 12

What do people come to you for, even when you haven't offered?

The friend who always calls you for advice on something specific. The colleague who keeps pulling you into their project. The thing people assume you do professionally even when you don't. That pattern is data.

You probably do and you've just stopped noticing it. People rarely name their own strengths accurately.

The Three Thank-Yous Exercise: Think of the last three times someone thanked you for something or said "you're so good at that." What were they thanking you for? Write those three things down. The overlap between them is your invisible skill set.

Question 4 of 12

What's already working?

Even if it feels small, partial, or accidental. What's gaining traction? What do people respond to? What feels easiest?

Question 5 of 12

What have you tried that didn't work (or felt wrong)?

Marketing approaches, business models, partnerships, pricing experiments. Anything you invested time in that landed flat or made you want to quit. Knowing what didn't work is as useful as knowing what did.

That is more common than you think. And honestly, it usually means you've been resourceful, not scattered.

Quick sort: List three things you tried. For each one, write whether it failed because of the idea itself, the timing, the execution, or because it just wasn't you. Most "failures" are timing or execution problems, not idea problems. We can sort through this together.

Bonus question (for anyone who already has something running)

What do you already have in place?

Products or offers, prices, where you sell or show up right now (shops, platforms, markets, social), and who actually buys. Rough is fine, and "not much yet" is a real answer.

Direction
Question 6 of 12

What are you walking away from?

A career, a way of working, a version of yourself, an industry. Sometimes the clearest compass is knowing what you're done with. And if you're grieving a thing you also loved? That's real and it belongs here too.

If you're in transition, this one might carry some weight. Leaving something you were good at (or that defined you for a long time) involves real loss, even when you're relieved. Herminia Ibarra's research on career transitions says identity shifts take longer than skill shifts. You're not behind. You're in the middle of a real thing.
Question 7 of 12

Who do you want to help?

Not a marketing persona. An actual type of person. Describe them like you're telling a friend about someone you met who you wish you could work with.

If you're not sure yet, think about the people who've already benefited from your help (even casually). Who felt like your people? Who energized you instead of draining you?

It's not. But it's also the sign of someone who genuinely cares, so let's work with it instead of against it.

Three quick tests:

Coffee test: Who would you happily spend an hour with for free, just talking about their situation?

Protection test: Who makes you angry on their behalf when they get bad advice?

Mirror test: Who reminds you of an earlier version of yourself?

The overlap between those three answers is your audience. Write whatever came up.

Question 8 of 12

What will you absolutely not do?

Boundaries are strategy. What's off the table? Types of work, types of clients, ways of working, schedules, compromises. The non-negotiables.

Question 9 of 12

What does your actual capacity look like right now?

Hours per week you can realistically dedicate to this. Energy levels (honestly). Other commitments pulling at you. Health stuff, family stuff, life stuff. I'm not asking so I can push you harder. I'm asking so I can build a plan your body can actually sustain.

If capacity feels like a loaded question right now, that's information too. Write what's real, not what you think you should be able to handle.

Welcome to the club. Fluctuating capacity is not a character flaw. It is a design constraint, and good strategy accounts for it.

The Capacity Audit: Think about the last two weeks. How many hours did you actually spend on your work or project (not thinking about it, actually doing it)? What time of day were those hours? Were they chosen or squeezed in? That pattern is your real capacity baseline. Write it down.

Question 10 of 12

If one thing got clearer in our conversation, what would it be?

This is your win condition. If we nail this, the session was worth it.

That is a very good thing to know about yourself right now. Seriously. Most people are chasing a finish line they never chose.

The Magic Wand: If I could guarantee one thing would be clearer by the end of our session, and you didn't have to explain or justify it to anyone, what would it be? Write the first thing that comes to mind, before your brain starts editing.

Question 11 of 12

The money question.

What does this need to do for you, financially? What's your financial picture? Are you supporting yourself with this work already? Is there a runway? A day job funding the dream? Savings with a deadline? I'm not asking to judge your number. I'm asking because strategy without financial honesty is just daydreaming with a spreadsheet.

You are not alone in that, and it is not a weakness. Most people who care deeply about their work have a complicated relationship with charging for it. The Trauma of Money framework names this well: our money stories are usually inherited, not chosen.

One question: If you knew the number you wanted was completely reasonable, and you were allowed to want it, what would it be? You don't have to share it. Just let yourself know it. Then write whatever you're comfortable with above.
